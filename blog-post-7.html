The Electronic Disturbance by CAE, 1994

October 27, 2026

“If labor in one area resists corporate demands, an alternative labor pool is quickly found.“ p.21

This exact sentiment is why I hate living in NYC so much. I recently was browsing apartments for rent (not because I am ready to move, and perhaps moving in any capacity is just a far out dream in this economy anyway), and came across the same two-bedroom apartment I grew up in. Washington Heights, my beloved neighborhood, is now home to people paying upwards of $5,000 for the same two bedroom apartment where my sister and I shared a bed. I showed my dad and he was like “What the fuck? Why?” with such genuine confusion, neither of us could believe it. Was a new stove and fridge enough to say this exact same apartment was suddenly worth thousands more?

We started to complain about how new tenants in our old hood don’t even seem to stay for very long. The turnover on these apartments is high because you need to live with one or two strangers in order to afford it. But what we really all agreed on was that it didn’t matter whether they stayed a while. In fact, the landlords probably prefer the tenants don’t stay too long so they can hike the price again and again. Because, as the quote up top says, if one tenant resists, an alternative tenant is quickly found. Washington Heights is (suddenly) an incredibly desirable neighborhood to live in. I see TikToks all the time praising its charm and proximity to the busier parts of the city. So, bigger than just the private living space, the neighborhood becomes a commodity. Behind it, the social status of living in an area lowkey and desired.

And it isn’t just rising rent costs that make me connect this gentrification to being a form of nomadic power, with the power in the hands of those holding the land for ransom, it’s the marketing of the spaces we built, the vibe we created that people love so much that they want it for themselves and will pay whatever to get it. “No one but the commodity has rights in the mall.” (p.24). The neighborhood is being preserved as an aesthetic built from the labor and passion of people who are being purposely priced out.

Going back a bit, the “relinquishment of sovereignty” is mentioned multiple times and it relates to what I was saying about how even the newcomers can’t afford this which is why they live two or three strangers deep. Nomadic power creates an environment where people are kind of forced to participate in systems that limit their sovereignty. The choices new tenants are presented with already exist within a restrictive economic system: pay the rent, or find roommates, or move further out, or don’t live in this city at all. These “choices” all suck. The landlords have all the power and can adjust the prices however they want because, it’s true, there will always be someone out there looking and willing to pay. Resisting this is very difficult, your replacement has already been accounted for, they already know what number they’ll list next.
