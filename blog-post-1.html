In Defense of the Poor Image by Hito Steyerl, 2011

September 8, 2026

Hito Stereyl makes the argument that the hierarchy of image is connected to larger social and economic hierarchies, explaining that high-resolution images are usually associated with professional institutions, expensive productions, and prestige, while low-resolution is deemed less valuable. “Focus” represents class, extending beyond whether an image is technically clear, it can also represent who is given the resources and privilege to be seen clearly in the first place. The poor image challenges this hierarchy because its value comes from its ability to move, be copied, compressed, downloaded, shared, rather than from its resolution. Because it can be moved easier, poor images reach people and spaces that higher quality images may not, liberating poor images from just sitting in archives and cinemas, or beyond that, consumption. These ideas reminded me of an episode of Black Mirror titled “White Christmas”. In this episode, a technology previously developed gives people the ability to “block” one another from real life. Once someone is blocked, the blocker can no longer see the other person clearly. Instead, their body and any images of them appear as a blurred, gray, pixelated figure. The main character is blocked by his girlfriend, making him invisible to her and her to him. SPOILERS AHEAD: In the end, the true main character, played by John Hamm, is convicted of a violent crime and punished by being blocked by everyone. In relation to Stereyl’s point that “Focus is identified as a class position” (p.1), the episode offers an interesting commentary on crime, social status, and technological access. It takes the idea of visibility a step further by making the clarity of a person’s image directly connected to their morality and social worth. This visibility functions as a form of power.
