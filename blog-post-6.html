"An Ironic Dream of a Common Language for Women in the Integrated Circuit," and "The Informatics of Domination" From A Cyborg Manifesto by Donna Haraway, 1985

October 20, 2026

This text met me at a very gender abolitionist time in my life.

The lesbian identity has been a huge topic of conversation among queer internet groups recently. These conversations revolve around trans men in the lesbian community, transmasculine people, trans women, and who gets to identify as a lesbian. It feels like everyone is under fire except cis lesbians who adhere to what is traditionally expected from lesbians. Baggy clothes are cool, skirts are cool too, but apparently only if you date masculine people so society can still project its boy/girl expectations onto you. A lot of these conversations have been transphobic, transmisogynistic, and, when you dig deeper, super racist.

It started with a tweet coming across my feed of three pictures of a couple consisting of a femme nonbinary person and a transmasculine person who identifies as butch. The tweet says:

“lesbian couple that became so butch and so femme that they look like hetslop to the blind eye”

Many lesbians were upset in the comments, arguing that if people assume you are in a heterosexual relationship at first glance, then what purpose does the lesbian identity serve? This made me think about Haraway's argument that identities are “contradictory, partial, and strategic.” I agree, I don't think identity needs to be defined by how things look or by whether other people understand it. Their identity as a lesbian couple is valid regardless of how strangers perceive them. But this raises a question for me: if my identity doesn't need to be validated or agreed upon by everyone else, then what is the point of having an identity at all? What does identity mean if it isn't something we can collectively agree on?

Haraway challenges the idea that people naturally belong to one unified group simply because they share a category. She writes that “there is nothing about being ‘female’ that naturally binds women.” I think this is relevant because even within lesbian communities, there are different ideas about what a lesbian should look like, how they should act, and who they should be attracted to. Historically, lesbian identity has been shaped by different understandings of gender and sexuality, and it has never been free from exclusion. When people insist that lesbians must look a certain way or fit a particular definition of womanhood, they risk reproducing the same rigid expectations that we have historically resisted.

At the same time, I don't think we can pretend that recognition doesn't matter. We use identity labels to find each other, build community, and describe experiences that might otherwise feel isolating. If identity is personal, why do we care so much about how other people define it? Maybe because identity is personal, but it is also social. The way other people perceive us affects how we move through the world, and shared language can help us make sense of those experiences.

Haraway offers another possibility through her idea of “coalition — affinity, not identity.” To me, this suggests that people don't need to be exactly alike to build a community or work toward a shared goal. We can have different relationships to gender, sexuality, and womanhood without those differences making our identities meaningless. We might be so afraid of losing a clear definition that we keep trying to draw boundaries around it.
