Constituents of a Theory of the media by Hans Magnus Enzensberger, 1970

October 6, 2026

In order to mobilize, and eventually dismantle what obstructs us from actual liberty, we need to all agree on at least ONE thing. What is that thing, and how the hell do we get everyone to agree on it?

I agree that everyone should have the ability, access to the tech, the education, etc., to participate in producing and shaping the media that shapes our lives. Less representation of perspective and simply more perspective. Enzensberger keeps coming back to the idea that the focus needs to shift away from media itself, because we already all know the media is manipulated, all media is manipulated somehow, even more so in 2026 than 1970; we need to begin interrogating who has the power to manipulate our media, even further into his thesis, who gets to speak and who gets to listen. Technology as we have it, a tool for media production, keeps us separated from our political power because media produces the same class relations found in capitalism. He uses the Xerox as an example of a potentially democratic technology being limited by capitalist systems set in place to separate the ruling class from the proletariat. We could all become our own publishers if everyone had access to copy and fax machines. But they’re expensive, and “the property of a monopoly” (p.17), so we can’t.

Okay, if we all don’t have access, and we all don’t have power, what do we do? Well, I think mobilization (Enzensberger’s “way out”) has to start with the changing of how we see ourselves in the media production/consumption cycle. Right now, we see ourselves as just consumers even when we are participating- liking, reposting, commenting. We are operating under systems that we do not own. We often receive information online and don’t even question where it all came from, the number of likes and shares is the verifying authority that makes us feel part of a collective consciousness, but that’s literally an illusion. Usernames and profile pictures does not a real person make. In the same way, I don’t think a reaction is an action. Reactions have no real power here unless everyone has, I don’t know … the same reaction?

And even then, now what? Now we have all agreed that something is wrong but, again, now what do we do? Enzensberger is urging us to go from passive “receivers” to active participants, for us to understand our mediums and use them to communicate our ideas to others, organize with them, and decide altogether what we do next. Otherwise, we get to where we are now. With #BLM, for example. We get millions of people watching horrific videos of Black people being killed by police, we repost infographics, we walk the streets expressing our outrage, and the thing that we’re all angry about stays right where it is, untouched, unmoved, unjust.

So maybe what we need to agree on is this: those affected by a system should have the power to communicate, organize, and change it. Which I think is what people think American democracy is but … come on, lol. I think that sentiment allows room for disagreement, which is necessary because communication only means something when we’re able to respond to each other. We must create the conditions where we can actually do something with whatever we are communicating. And I question if the tech we have is enough to do that. I question if we are, collectively, able to do this, able to actually do something.
