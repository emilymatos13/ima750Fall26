Constituents of a Theory of the media by Hans Magnus Enzensberger, 1970

October 6, 2026

“The spectacle is not a collection of images but a social relation among people mediated by images” (p. 8). I understand the spectacle to be how images structure the way we understand other people and ourselves. We know people through their representations of themselves. Instead of experiencing someone’s actual life, we experience their life through their Instagram or TikTok account. We see what they eat, where they travel, what they buy, what they wear, etc., but we are still only seeing an edited or spliced together version of their life. This connects to Debord’s description of the spectacle as “the concrete inversion of life” and the “autonomous movement of the non-living” (p. 8). Life becomes something we observe and consume rather than something we actively participate in. The spectacle teaches us what life is supposed to look like. It gives us images of what is desirable, successful, beautiful, normal, or important, and then we measure our own lives against those images.

Debord, engaged in Marxist philosophy, argues that under capitalism, people are separated from their work, the product, and other people. Separated from our desires and control over our lives. “Separation is the alpha and the omega of the spectacle.” (p.15). The more society organizes itself around commodities and specialized forms of labor, the more disconnected we become from the processes that actually produce our lives. Social media intensifies this separation because so much of our experience is filtered through these representations. Instead of talking to someone directly, we watch their stories. Instead of experiencing an event, we watch someone else’s documentation of it. Instead of asking ourselves what we want, we can scroll through hundreds of images that tell us what other people want and have, and what we should want and have too. This connects directly to Debord’s statement that “the more he contemplates the less he lives” (p. 18). The more we look at representations of other people living, the more passive our own participation can become.

So what is the relationship between spectacle and desire? Capitalism creates conditions where certain things begin to feel necessary or desirable. “The principle of commodity fetishism... reaches its absolute fulfillment in the spectacle, where the tangible world is replaced by a selection of images which exist above it” (p. 22). An iPhone, for example, is obviously a tool, but it can also function as a representation of wealth, access, taste, or social status. The difference between a green text bubble and a blue one is socially meaningful. We are buying the object and what the object represents, and what we believe possessing it says about us.

Debord claims that “the spectacle is the moment when the commodity has attained the total occupation of social life” (p. 24). What interests me about this idea is how difficult it is to imagine what exists outside of the commodity system. If almost every part of life can be turned into something to sell, advertise, document, or consume, then even our desires will begin to feel like products. If the society around me is constantly telling me what is beautiful, successful, desirable, necessary, or worth wanting, then how do I know which desires actually come from me? If I want a certain pair of shoes because I genuinely like them, but I only learned to value them because I have seen them repeatedly on people I admire, where does my own desire begin and the spectacle’s influence end? Maybe Debord is suggesting that this boundary becomes increasingly difficult to locate. It becomes difficult to separate our desires from the social systems that have taught us how to desire in the first place.
